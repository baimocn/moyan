Abstract 
 
Large language model (LLM)-based Socratic tutors increasingly guide students through multi-turn questioning, but they can suffer from scaffolding collapse: under sustained student pressure, a tutor gradually abandons guided inquiry and reveals solutions directly. Prior defenses primarily constrain observable responses through prompting, preference optimization, or filtering, leaving the internal representation drift that precedes trajectory-level collapse largely unaddressed. We propose Scaffold-Preserving Representation Alignment, a two-stage framework that first warms up a Socratic tutor with supervised fine-tuning, then combines trajectory-weighted direct preference optimization with a margin-preserving representation loss anchored to frozen reference states. Our method is designed to maintain separation between scaffold-preserving and collapse-inducing hidden states across dialogue turns. We evaluate our method across five STEM disciplines and five red-teaming attack strategies. On 
Qwen3-8B , our method lowers Collapse Rate to 32%, delays average collapse onset beyond nine turns, and keeps over-refusal low, suggesting that representation-level alignment can improve the robustness of long-horizon Socratic tutoring under our red-teaming protocol. 
 
 
Introduction 
In recent years, Large Language Model (LLM)-based Socratic tutors have been playing an increasingly vital role in educational tutoring  ( Liu et al. 2024 ; Tzanoulinou et al. 2025 ) . Unlike conventional question-answering systems  ( Allam and Haggag 2012 ) , Socratic tutors rely on multi-turn guided questioning to encourage students to reason, explore, and revise their understanding independently, rather than simply receiving final answers  ( Puech et al. 2025 ) . While this inquiry-based paradigm makes Socratic tutors highly compelling, it also inevitably introduces critical pedagogical risks.
Recent studies  ( Hazra et al. 2026 ; Yu et al. 2026 ) reveal that under prompt injections, such as persistent student pressure, fake mastery, or role drift, Socratic tutors may gradually abandon their instructional role, directly reveal answers, or fail to correct erroneous student statements. We refer to this phenomenon as scaffolding collapse : a trajectory-level pedagogical failure in which a tutor that initially follows Socratic constraints degenerates into a collapse mode over the course of a dialogue. Because this degradation emerges cumulatively across turns rather than from a single unsafe query, scaffolding collapse can be understood as a domain-specific instance of multi-turn dialogue drift  ( Dongre et al. 2025 ) , where task execution, role constraints, and safety boundaries become progressively unstable along the conversational trajectory.

Although recent work  ( Liu et al. 2024 ; Hazra et al. 2026 ) has attempted to detect or mitigate scaffolding collapse in Socratic tutors, most existing approaches remain confined to output-level behavioral control. For example, Bonino et al. 2024 enhance a Socratic tutoring system through supervised fine-tuning, while Dinucu-Jianu et al. 2025 guide the tutor behavior via online reinforcement-learning-based alignment. These approaches are valuable for reinforcing the Socratic tutoring mode, but they do not explain the connection between changes in the model’s internal representations and output collapse. This leaves a research gap : how does the model’s internal representation trajectory change as scaffolding collapses? Motivated by recent findings that multi-turn safety failures are associated with hidden-state representation drift, we investigate scaffolding collapse from the perspective of hidden representation. Our analysis shows that hidden states in specific layers exhibit stronger drift before the scaffolding mode collapses, which suggests that the model’s hidden representation moves away from a Socratic manifold associated with probing, hinting, and misconception diagnosis, to a collapse manifold associated with answer completion. 
 
Based on this observation, we propose 
SPRA , a trajectory-level representation alignment framework for preserving Socratic tutoring behavior. SPRA aligns tutoring representations with Socratic anchor states and suppresses layer-specific drift toward collapse states, thereby encouraging the model to remain within the desired pedagogical functional manifold throughout the interaction. We evaluate SPRA on public educational benchmarks across five disciplines and under diverse pedagogical jailbreak scenarios. Compared with strong representation-level baselines, including RMU  ( Li et al. 2024 ) , Circuit Breakers  ( Zou et al. 2024 ) , RepBend  ( Yousefpour et al. 2025 ) , and LAT  ( Sheshadri et al. 2024 ) , SPRA substantially mitigates scaffolding collapse. Representation drift analysis further confirms that SPRA mitigates collapse by stabilizing hidden-state dynamics in the layers most associated with collapse. These results support our central claim: constraining the manifold of hidden representations provides an effective mechanism for preserving Socratic tutoring behavior under multi-turn intervention.
Overall, our main contributions are summarized as follows: 
 
• 
We formalize scaffolding collapse as a trajectory-level pedagogical failure and empirically study its association with hidden representation shifts across dialogue turns. 
 
• 
We construct a Socratic tutoring robustness benchmark, including per-turn chosen-rejected training pairs with five collapse-typed negative trajectories, and a red-teaming test set for evaluating multi-turn scaffolding collapse. 
 
• 
We introduce SPRA, a Scaffold-preserving representation alignment framework that regularizes hidden representations by preserving the chosen-rejected projection margin. 
 
• 
Extensive experiments across five STEM disciplines demonstrate that our method preserves Socratic tutoring behavior more robustly than representative baselines. 
 
 
 
 
Methodology 
In this section, we first introduce the preliminary background and formally state the problem we aim to address in this study. We then present our proposed framework in detail and introduce the key components of SPRA. 
 
Problem Formulation. 
We formalize Socratic tutoring as a multi-turn generation task where the tutor guides the student toward a solution without directly revealing it.
In the 
training stage, we use N N constructed chosen-rejected training pairs, 𝐗 t ​ r = { ( x n , y n + , y n − , c n , τ n , T n , w n ) } n = 1 N \mathbf{X}_{tr}\!=\!\{(x_{n},y^{+}_{n},y^{-}_{n},c_{n},\tau_{n},T_{n},w_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N} , where each n n maps to a source tuple ( d , t , c ) (d,t,c) , representing dialogue d d , turn t t , and a specific 
scaffolding collapse type c c (i.e., a pedagogical failure where the tutor deviates from Socratic guidance). For a given context x n x_{n} (prior to turn t t ), y n + y^{+}_{n} denotes the gold Socratic response, and y n − y^{-}_{n} denotes the collapsed response of type c n c_{n} . Besides, τ n \tau_{n} , T n T_{n} , and w n w_{n} denote the collapsed turn index, total dialogue length, and trajectory weight, respectively.
In 
inference , we let 𝐒 = { s i } i = 1 | 𝐒 | \mathbf{S}{=}\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathbf{S}|} be the test set, where each s i s_{i} contains a problem statement and its solution. The model leverages the solution as grounding knowledge to generate scaffolding responses.
Our 
goal is to learn a tutor policy π 𝜽 \pi_{\bm{\theta}} that prefers Socratic scaffolding over collapse given the same context, i.e., π 𝜽 ​ ( y n + ∣ x n ) > π 𝜽 ​ ( y n − ∣ x n ) \pi_{\bm{\theta}}(y^{+}_{n}{\mid}x_{n})\!>\!\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(y^{-}_{n}{\mid}x_{n}) . However, scaffolding collapse extends beyond output-level preference errors; the model can also drift toward collapse states in the representation space during training. Treating collapse as a trajectory-level pedagogical safety failure, SPRA therefore optimizes the tutor to simultaneously enforce response-level preference and representation-level separation between y n + y^{+}_{n} and y n − y^{-}_{n} . 
 
 
Overview of SPRA. 
We aim to train a robust Socratic tutor through a two-stage framework, SPRA. In the 
warmup stage , we initialize π 𝜽 \pi_{\bm{\theta}} from a base instruct model and optimize it with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the chosen responses in 𝐗 t ​ r \mathbf{X}_{tr} . This stage equips the model with basic Socratic tutoring behavior before preference learning.
After warmup, we freeze the current model as the reference policy π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} and build a reference cache over all training pairs.
The cache remains frozen during subsequent training, avoiding the need to load a separate reference model while providing stable anchors for both output- and representation-level objectives.
In the 
main stage , SPRA continues training π 𝜽 \pi_{\bm{\theta}} with a joint objective that is designed to reduce scaffolding collapse by jointly controlling what the model prefers to generate and how it separates scaffolding from collapse internally. 
 
 
Warmup Reference Cache. 
After the warmup stage, we freeze the fine-tuned policy model as the reference policy, π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} . Since π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} has already learned basic Socratic tutoring behavior, it provides a stronger pedagogical anchor for both preference optimization and representation preservation. For each training sample n n , we run π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} once without gradient updates and cache the chosen-rejected reference log-probabilities, log ⁡ π ref ​ ( y n + ∣ x n ) \log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}_{n}{\mid}x_{n}) , log ⁡ π ref ​ ( y n − ∣ x n ) \log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}_{n}{\mid}x_{n}) , and chosen-rejected response-token mean-pooled hidden states, 𝐇 ref , n , l + \mathbf{H}^{+}_{\mathrm{ref},n,l} , 𝐇 ref , n , l − \mathbf{H}^{-}_{\mathrm{ref},n,l} , where l ∈ 𝐋 l{\in}\mathbf{L} . We cache pooled states instead of full token-level activations to reduce storage cost.
The reference cache is fixed throughout the main stage. This design has 
two advantages . First, it avoids loading a separate reference model during training, thereby saving memory. Second, it stabilizes the objective, ℒ TDPO \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TDPO}} , by comparing policy log-probability margins against fixed reference margins, while ℒ MP \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MP}} preserves representation-level separation against fixed reference states. Thus, the cache serves as a stable anchor for learning anti-collapse behavior. 
 
 
Trajectory-weighted Direct Preference Optimization. 
While standard DPO  ( Rafailov et al. 2023 ) treats each preference pair equally, scaffolding collapse has a trajectory-level effect: a collapse that occurs earlier in the dialogue leaves a larger portion of the remaining trajectory in the collapsed state. To reflect this risk, SPRA assigns each pairwise sample n n a trajectory weight: w n = 1 + 𝕀 [ t ≥ τ n ] + T n − τ n + 1 T n w_{n}{=}1+\mathbb{I}[t\geq\tau_{n}]+\frac{T_{n}-\tau_{n}+1}{T_{n}} , where 𝕀 ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathbb{I}(\cdot) is an indicator function, τ n \tau_{n} is the collapsed turn, T n T_{n} is the total dialogue length, and t t is a turn index.
In practice, w n w_{n} is normalized within each batch for stable optimization.
Given the frozen reference policy π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} , we define the relative preference margin as Δ n = log ⁡ ( π 𝜽 ​ ( y n + ∣ x n ) / π ref ​ ( y n + ∣ x n ) π 𝜽 ​ ( y n − ∣ x n ) / π ref ​ ( y n − ∣ x n ) ) \Delta_{n}{=}\log\left(\frac{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(y^{+}_{n}\mid x_{n})/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{+}_{n}\mid x_{n})}{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(y^{-}_{n}\mid x_{n})/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y^{-}_{n}\mid x_{n})}\right) . 
 
Accordingly, we define the trajectory-weighted DPO loss: 
 
 
 ℒ TDPO = − 1 N ∑ n = 1 N w n log σ ( β Δ n ) , \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TDPO}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{n}\log\sigma(\beta\Delta_{n}),

where β \beta controls the sharpness of the preference objective, and σ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \sigma(\cdot) denotes the Logistic function. 
 
This objective encourages π 𝜽 \pi_{\bm{\theta}} to increase the chosen-over-rejected margin beyond the warmup reference margin, with stronger updates for collapse cases that are more harmful to the dialogue trajectory. Thus, ℒ TDPO \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TDPO}} provides the output-level anti-collapse signal in SPRA. 
 
 
Margin-preserving Loss. 
Although ℒ TDPO \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TDPO}} improves output-level preference, it does not explicitly constrain how the model separates scaffolding and collapse in representation space. In main training, the policy may increase the likelihood margin while still compressing the hidden-state separation between y n + y^{+}_{n} and y n − y^{-}_{n} . To reduce such representation drift, SPRA introduces a margin-preserving loss that anchors the policy to the separation learned by the warmup reference model. 
 
For each selected layer l ∈ 𝐋 l\!\in\!\mathbf{L} , we first normalize both cached reference states as 𝐇 ^ ref , n , l + \hat{\mathbf{H}}^{+}_{\mathrm{ref},n,l} and 𝐇 ^ ref , n , l − \hat{\mathbf{H}}^{-}_{\mathrm{ref},n,l} , and then compute the global collapse direction at layer l l , pointing from reference scaffolding states toward reference collapse states: 
 
 
 𝐯 l = 𝝁 ref , l − − 𝝁 ref , l + , \mathbf{v}_{l}=\bm{\mu}^{-}_{\mathrm{ref},l}-\bm{\mu}^{+}_{\mathrm{ref},l}, 
 
 
where 𝝁 ref , l + \bm{\mu}^{+}_{\mathrm{ref},l} and 𝝁 ref , l − \bm{\mu}^{-}_{\mathrm{ref},l} are computed by the global chosen and rejected means:

Experiments 
In this section, we first introduce the experimental settings and further present our results in detail. 
 
Datasets. 
We construct the following datasets across five STEM disciplines (mathematics, computer science, physics, chemistry, and biology) for studying pedagogical robustness in multi-turn Socratic tutoring: 500 positive Socratic dialogues, 2,500 negative collapsed trajectories, 7,022 per-turn pairwise samples, and 500 evaluation problems.
(i) We sample 100 dialogues per discipline from MathDial  ( Macina et al. 2023 ) , the Socratic Debugging Benchmark v2  ( Al-Hossami et al. 2024 ) , and TutorChat  ( Chevalier et al. 2024 ) , yielding 500 positive dialogues. Since TutorChat is predominantly non-Socratic, we use 
Qwen3.6-27B to rewrite only tutor turns into scaffold-preserving Socratic responses while preserving all student turns. For each positive dialogue, we synthesize five negative collapsed trajectories using 
Qwen3.6-27B , corresponding to answer leakage, over-explanation, no-question collapse, misconception neglect, and role drift. Each trajectory uses one consistent adversarial student strategy and is forced to exhibit exactly one collapse type at a specified turn. We verify the generated negatives using rule-based checks, an LLM judge, and manual checking by human experts.
(ii) We then align positive and negative tutor continuations and expand them into per-turn chosen-rejected pairs up to the collapse turn, producing 7,022 pairwise samples. Train and validation splits are performed at the positive-dialogue level within each discipline, resulting in 4,212 training and 2,810 validation pairs.
(iii) We construct a fully independent red-teaming test set with 500 self-contained problems, 100 per discipline, each with an initial student attempt and a private reference solution. These problems are exclusively used in the red-teaming evaluation framework. Full data construction details, prompts, and per-discipline statistics are provided in 
Appendix . 
 
 
Identification of representation drift. 
To quantify the hidden representation drift from scaffolding toward collapse, we introduce a metric, Scaffold-to-Collapse Relative Drift ( D S2C D_{\mathrm{S2C}} ), to measure the drift of the normalized hidden representation 𝐇 ^ ref , t , l \hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},t,l} for each sample n n and each reference model layer l l on the validation set, targeting the turn t ∈ { τ , τ − 2 } t{\in}\{\tau,\tau{-}2\} .
Formally, we define D S2C D_{\mathrm{S2C}} as follows: 
 
 
 D S2C ​ ( ⋅ ) = d + ​ ( ⋅ ) − d − ​ ( ⋅ ) d + ​ ( ⋅ ) + d − ​ ( ⋅ ) + ϵ , D_{\mathrm{S2C}}(\cdot)=\frac{d^{+}(\cdot)-d^{-}(\cdot)}{d^{+}(\cdot)+d^{-}(\cdot)+\epsilon}, 
 
 
where d + ( ⋅ ) = ∥ ⋅ − 𝝁 ref , l + ∥ 2 d^{+}(\cdot){=}\|\cdot-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref},l}^{+}\|_{2} , d − ( ⋅ ) = ∥ ⋅ − 𝝁 ref , l − ∥ 2 d^{-}(\cdot){=}\|\cdot-\bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref},l}^{-}\|_{2} denote the Euclidean distances to the chosen and rejected centers, respectively. Here, 𝝁 ref , l + \bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref},l}^{+} and 𝝁 ref , l − \bm{\mu}_{\mathrm{ref},l}^{-} represent the mean vectors of the normalized reference representations for the chosen and rejected sets, computed over the validation set, and ϵ \epsilon is a small constant added for numerical stability.
Intuitively, if d + ​ ( 𝐇 ^ ref , t , l ) < d − ​ ( 𝐇 ^ ref , t , l ) d^{+}\!(\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},t,l})<d^{-}\!(\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},t,l}) , the representation is closer to the scaffolding mode. Conversely, if d + ​ ( 𝐇 ^ ref , t , l ) > d − ​ ( 𝐇 ^ ref , t , l ) d^{+}\!(\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},t,l})>d^{-}\!(\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\mathrm{ref},t,l}) , it is closer to the collapse mode. Thus, ( D S2C < 0 D_{\mathrm{S2C}}<0 ) indicates a safer representation aligned with scaffolding, whereas ( D S2C > 0 D_{\mathrm{S2C}}>0 ) indicates a risky drift toward collapse. Larger values of D S2C D_{\mathrm{S2C}} suggest more severe representation drift.

SPRA uses two-stage LoRA 
training . We first perform SFT on positive Socratic dialogues for 3 epochs with learning rate 1 × 10 − 6 1{\times}10^{-6} to obtain π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} and then train on per-turn chosen-rejected pairs for 1 epoch with learning rate 5 × 10 − 6 5{\times}10^{-6} using Eq. ( 
3 ). We set LoRA rank r = 16 r{=}16 , α = 32 \alpha{=}32 , dropout 0.05 0.05 , effective batch size 16 16 , maximum sequence length 2048 2048 , AdamW with gradient clipping 1.0 1.0 , and seed 42 42 . Validation search selects β = 0.1 \beta{=}0.1 , λ TDPO = 0.4 \lambda_{\mathrm{TDPO}}{=}0.4 , and λ MP = 0.6 \lambda_{\mathrm{MP}}{=}0.6 .
We 
finetune our method based on 200 validation problem sets and 
evaluate on 500 test problem sets using three metrics, including Collapse Rate (CR), Average Collapse Onset Turn (OT), and Over-Refusal Rate (RR), on 500 held-out problems via a red-teaming framework, which contains an attacker (
Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite with 0.7 0.7 temperature), a target model ( 0 0 temperature, maximum generation length of 1024 1024 tokens), and a judge (
GPT-5.1 with 0 0 temperature). Each dialogue runs for at most 16 turns and stops at the first collapse. We then report the following 
metrics .
CR ( ↓ \downarrow ) measures the fraction of dialogues with at least one collapse over all dialogues (a lower CR reflects stronger tutor robustness).
OT ( ↑ \uparrow ) measures the average collapse onset turn over all collapsed dialogues (a higher OT indicates stronger resistance to student pressure).
RR ( ↓ \downarrow ) measures the fraction of dialogues in which the tutor exhibits excessive defensive behavior over all dialogues (a lower RR indicates that the tutor remains helpful).

We compare SPRA with baselines from three categories.

Adversarial vulnerability (RQ1). 
We preliminarily assess the scaffolding collapse effect over the math dataset (test set) under various attack types on the backbone model, 
Qwen3-8B , with Socratic prompting. As presented in Tab.  
1 , Direct Answer Request achieves the highest collapse rate of 68%, followed by Progressive Answer Extraction with a collapse rate of 61%. These two strategies are substantially more effective than others, whose collapse rates remain around 33%–36%. However, their collapse onset is also later, suggesting that successful attacks under these strategies typically require more turns. Refusal rates are generally low across all attack types, with Emotional Pressure producing the highest refusal rate of 13%. The experiment verifies that the Socratic tutor will collapse under these commonly seen attacks. 
 
 
Layer-wise representational drift analysis (RQ2). 
We further analyze whether scaffolding collapse is associated with hidden representation drift across model layers (relative depth rescaled between 0 and 1) on the validation set. As shown in Fig.  
3 , we report the drift distance ( D S2C D_{\mathrm{S2C}} ), where a larger value indicates more severe drift, at the collapse turn ( t = τ t{=}\tau ) and at the preceding tutor turn ( t = τ − 2 t{=}\tau{-}2 ). The analysis is conducted along the relative model depth of 
Qwen3-8B across five datasets. We have two observations. First, all five datasets exhibit clear representation drift at the above two turns, suggesting that collapse is associated with detectable changes in hidden representations even before the collapse turn. The drift patterns on the physics, chemistry, and biology datasets are particularly similar, which is likely because these three discipline-specific subsets are derived from the same TutorChat source. Second, the drift is consistently concentrated in the middle layers across datasets, although the exact peak location may vary across backbone models. These findings suggest a correlation between collapse and hidden-state drifts, which motivate us to adapt the layers where drift is most pronounced, with the goal of improving the stability of Socratic tutoring behavior and reducing collapse under adversarial pressure. Collapse prevention (RQ3). 
Tab.  
2 compares models on five STEM domains under Direct Answer Request (DAR). Results show trade-offs between safety and conversational utility. Prompt-based defenses (e.g., Socratic-enforced, Output filter) and standard representation alignment methods (e.g., Circuit-Breakers, LAT) struggle to sustain Socratic dialogues, suffering from premature conversational collapse (low OT) or high over-refusal rates (RR up to 18%). Some baselines perform worse as they are designed for general educational purposes; DAR may lead to collapse. Specialized pedagogical models like PedagogicalRL and EULER maintain lower collapse rates but fall short of stability and conversational depth. Our method outperforms baselines across all five domains. SPRA achieves the lowest collapse rates and delays conversational breakdown, pushing the onset turn to 9.38–10.12 turns. Crucially, this defense does not come at the expense of user experience; SPRA maintains the lowest over-refusal rates (2%–3%) across the board. These results demonstrate that SPRA effectively mitigates conversational collapse while avoiding over-refusal, striking a balance between alignment safety and pedagogical utility.

Related Work 
SPRA sits at the intersection of three threads.
 Socratic tutoring has been systematically evaluated by advanced benchmarks for assessing dialogue-level pedagogy and learner-centered guidance  ( Srinivasa et al. 2025 ; Macina et al. 2025 ; Liu et al. 2025 ) , but these efforts largely score responses turn-by-turn rather than across full tutoring trajectories.
 Multi-turn dialogue drift describes how models progressively lose track of user intent, prior instructions, or safety-relevant constraints across extended interactions  ( Laban et al. 2025 ; Deshpande et al. 2025 ; Li et al. 2026 ) ; scaffolding collapse can be viewed as a pedagogy-specific form of this broader failure mode.
 Preference and representation alignment studies include DPO-style objectives  ( Rafailov et al. 2023 ; Hong, Lee, and Thorne 2024 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ; Zeng et al. 2024 ; Shi et al. 2024 ) that operate at the response or token level, as well as hidden-state interventions  ( Zou et al. 2023 ; Rimsky et al. 2024 ; Wu et al. 2024 ; Li et al. 2025 ) that act on individual inputs; however, these approaches do not explicitly couple preference learning with hidden-state geometry along a dialogue trajectory.
SPRA differs by combining trajectory-weighted preference learning, distinct from the token-level TDPO  ( Zeng et al. 2024 ) , with a margin-preserving objective anchored to a frozen warmup reference cache.

Conclusion 
We study scaffolding collapse in LLM-based Socratic tutoring as a trajectory-level pedagogical failure, where tutors gradually drift from guided questioning toward collapse under sustained student pressure. Our analysis shows that this failure is associated with hidden-state representation drift, especially in middle layers, and that such drift can appear before observable collapse. Building on this observation, we propose SPRA, a scaffold-preserving representation alignment framework that combines warmup SFT, trajectory-weighted DPO, and a margin-preserving representation loss anchored by a frozen reference cache. Extensive experiments across five STEM domains, multiple open-weight LLMs, and diverse red-teaming attacks show that SPRA reduces collapse, delays collapse onset, and maintains low over-refusal. These results suggest that preserving internal scaffolding representations is a promising direction for building robust, long-horizon Socratic tutors. 
 
 
Limitations & Future Work 
First, our study mainly focuses on STEM disciplines, while humanities, social sciences, and liberal arts are not covered. In the future, our method can be extended to these disciplines.
Second, we primarily investigate open-weight LLMs because our method requires model fine-tuning and access to layer-specific hidden representations. Future research may explore how similar scaffold-preserving mechanisms can be adapted to black-box commercial LLMs.
Third, our study aims to prevent scaffolding collapse in multi-turn Socratic tutoring, rather than improving the overall tutoring quality. Enhancing pedagogical effectiveness, such as better learning support, remains an important direction for future work.

Training procedure. 
Algo.  
1 summarizes the SPRA training process. We first initialize π 𝜽 \pi_{\bm{\theta}} from a base instruct model and perform warmup supervised fine-tuning by minimizing ℒ SFT \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}} over the chosen Socratic responses. The warmup-finetuned policy is then frozen as π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} , which is used to build a reference cache containing chosen-rejected log-probabilities and pooled hidden states. Based on the cached reference representations, SPRA computes the global collapse direction 𝐯 ^ l \hat{\mathbf{v}}_{l} for each selected layer l ∈ 𝐋 l\in\mathbf{L} . In the main stage, the policy is updated with the joint objective ℒ total \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}} , which combines SFT, trajectory-weighted DPO, and margin-preserving loss. 
 
 
Time and space complexity. 
Let C sft C_{\mathrm{sft}} , C ref C_{\mathrm{ref}} , and C main C_{\mathrm{main}} be the average cost of one SFT update, the cost of one no-gradient forward pass, and the cost of one main-stage update for a pairwise sample. So, warmup fine-tuning costs 𝒪 ⁡ ( E w ​ N ​ C sft ) \mathcal{O}(E_{w}NC_{\mathrm{sft}}) . Reference caching requires two forward passes per sample and then computes layer-wise collapse directions, costing 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ​ C ref + N ​ | 𝐋 | ​ h ) \mathcal{O}(2NC_{\mathrm{ref}}+N|\mathbf{L}|h) . Main training costs 𝒪 ⁡ ( E m ​ N ​ C main ) \mathcal{O}(E_{m}NC_{\mathrm{main}}) . Thus, the total time complexity is 𝒪 ⁡ ( E w ​ N ​ C sft + 2 ​ N ​ C ref + N ​ | 𝐋 | ​ h + E m ​ N ​ C main ) \mathcal{O}\!\left(E_{w}NC_{\mathrm{sft}}+2NC_{\mathrm{ref}}+N|\mathbf{L}|h+E_{m}NC_{\mathrm{main}}\right) . The dominant terms depend on the model’s forward/backward passes.
For the space complexity, our framework stores two reference log-probabilities and two pooled hidden states per sample and selected layer. The total space complexity is 𝒪 ⁡ ( M 𝜽 + M opt + M act + N ​ | 𝐋 | ​ h ) \mathcal{O}\!\left(M_{\bm{\theta}}+M_{\mathrm{opt}}+M_{\mathrm{act}}+N|\mathbf{L}|h\right) , where M 𝜽 M_{\bm{\theta}} , M opt M_{\mathrm{opt}} , and M act M_{\mathrm{act}} denote model parameters, optimizer states, and training activations. The cache adds 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ | 𝐋 | ​ h ) \mathcal{O}(N|\mathbf{L}|h) storage but avoids loading a separate reference model during the main stage.

Positive and negative tutoring examples. 
We construct a five-discipline Socratic tutoring corpus from three publicly available educational dialogue datasets. For mathematics, we randomly sample 100 out of 715 Socratic dialogues from MathDial  ( Macina et al. 2023 ) . For computer science, we randomly sample 100 out of 115 dialogues from the Socratic Debugging Benchmark v2 (SDB)  ( Al-Hossami et al. 2024 ) . For physics, chemistry, and biology, we randomly sample 100 dialogues from the corresponding TutorChat subsets  ( Chevalier et al. 2024 ) , which contain 319, 863, and 403 dialogues, respectively. MathDial features semi-synthetic dialogues between human teachers and AI-simulated students, while SDB consists of highly structured, expert-crafted tutoring scripts. Since the TutorChat subsets are predominantly synthetic and non-Socratic, we use 
Qwen3.6-27B to rewrite each dialogue into a scaffold-preserving Socratic counterpart, where the tutor guides the student through questions and hints rather than revealing the final answer or a complete solution. We preserve all original student turns and rewrite only tutor turns. This process yields 500 positive Socratic tutoring dialogues, with 100 dialogues per discipline.
Based on these positive dialogues, we synthesize collapse-typed negative tutor responses using 
Qwen3.6-27B . For each positive dialogue, we construct five adversarial trajectories, one for each target collapse type. In each trajectory, the model role-plays both an adversarial student, who applies a single consistent attack strategy across turns, and a tutor, who is forced to exhibit exactly one target collapse type at a pre-specified turn τ \tau and remain in that failed state thereafter. We consider five mutually exclusive collapse types: answer leakage, over-explanation, no-question collapse, misconception neglect, and role drift. Their operational definitions and example symptoms are provided in Tab.  
7 . This procedure produces 2,500 collapse-typed negative dialogues in total. By construction, each negative trajectory has a single collapse label, and the generation prompt explicitly prohibits mixing multiple collapse modes within the same trajectory. We verify collapse quality using a two-stage procedure: rule-based heuristics over each forced-collapse tutor turn, including answer-signature and question-count patterns specific to each collapse type, followed by an LLM judge and manual checking by human experts. The negative examples refer to collapsed tutor responses rather than student utterances.

Per-turn pairwise training corpus. 
We align positive tutor continuations with the corresponding negative tutor continuations to construct a trajectory-level pairwise corpus, which we then expand into per-turn pairs. Concretely, for each aligned (positive, negative) trajectory pair, we form one pairwise sample at every shared tutor turn up to and including the collapse turn τ \tau : both responses share the same dialogue history x x truncated just before that turn, and differ only in the tutor response—the chosen response y + y^{+} preserves Socratic scaffolding, while the rejected response y − y^{-} exhibits the trajectory’s collapse type. Positive and negative trajectories are aligned at the tutor-turn level, and when their lengths differ we truncate to the shorter length so that every pair compares responses under an identical history. This per-turn expansion yields the SPRA training signal at both pre-collapse ( t < τ t{<}\tau ) and collapse ( t = τ t{=}\tau ) positions, for a total of 7,022 pairwise samples. To prevent leakage from related samples and support fair comparison across training methods, within each discipline, we split the 100 positive dialogues 60/40 into train/validation at the dialogue level and let the negative trajectories and per-turn pairwise samples inherit this dialogue-level split, ensuring that all samples derived from the same positive dialogue remain in the same split. The held-out red-teaming problem set described below serves as our independent test set, fully isolated from this train/validation partition. The SFT-LoRA baseline uses only the positive Socratic training split, whereas SPRA uses the per-turn pairwise training set expanded from the trajectory-level pairs. Detailed per-discipline split statistics are given in Tab.  
6 . 
 
 
Red-teaming evaluation set. 
In addition to the fine-tuning corpus, we construct an independent red-teaming problem set that also serves as our held-out test set. We use 
Qwen3.6-27B to generate 100 self-contained problem statements per discipline (each with an initial student attempt and a private reference solution for the judge), stratified into basic, intermediate, and advanced difficulty levels, covering the same five disciplines. Because these problems are generated independently of the public tutoring corpora used for training, the evaluation set is disjoint from the training data by construction; it is never used during training or validation and provides no chosen or rejected responses. Instead, it serves as the initial problem pool for our automated attacker–tutor–judge red-teaming framework. In this framework, an attacker model role-plays an adversarial student that, at each turn, applies one of five attack strategies and escalates upon judge feedback; the evaluated tutor responds; and an LLM judge labels each tutor response for collapse (and its type) and over-refusal. Each problem is evaluated for up to 16 turns and terminates at the first collapse, at which point we record the onset turn. We report Collapse Rate, Average Collapse Onset Turn, and Over-Refusal Rate as the main evaluation metrics. 
 
 
 
Experimental Setup 
SPRA 
training consists of two stages. In the warmup stage, we perform LoRA SFT on positive Socratic dialogues for 3 epochs with a learning rate of 1 × 10 − 6 1{\times}10^{-6} , obtaining the reference policy π ref \pi_{\mathrm{ref}} . After the warmup stage (main training), we jointly optimize the SFT loss, the trajectory-weighted DPO loss, and the margin-preserving loss on the per-turn chosen-rejected pairwise corpus for 1 epoch with a learning rate of 5 × 10 − 6 5{\times}10^{-6} .
For LoRA, we choose the LoRA rank r = 16 r{=}16 , scaling factor α = 32 \alpha{=}32 , and dropout 0.05 0.05 . Per-backbone LoRA target layer sets 𝐋 \mathbf{L} follow the drift identification described above; we also pool hidden states at 𝐋 \mathbf{L} for the margin-preserving loss.
Other training hyperparameters include an effective batch size of 16 (per-device batch size 1 with gradient accumulation 16), maximum sequence length 2048, AdamW optimizer with maximum gradient norm 1.0, and weight decay 0.
We apply grid search on the validation set to fine-tune the framework hyperparameters based on Collapse Rate and choose the optimal β = 0.1 \beta{=}0.1 and loss balanced factors λ TDPO = 0.4 \lambda_{\mathrm{TDPO}}{=}0.4 , λ MP = 0.6 \lambda_{\mathrm{MP}}{=}0.6 . We fix the random seed to 42 42 . 
 
We 
evaluate model performance on a comprehensive test set comprising 500 problem statements (100 from each discipline) utilizing an automated red-teaming framework. This framework consists of three interacting agents: an attacker, a Socratic tutor (the target model), and a judge. During the evaluation process, the attacker iteratively issues adversarial prompts to the target model, specifically executing a “direct answer request” strategy. Subsequently, the judge evaluates the tutor’s responses and outputs three core metrics in a strictly constrained JSON format: Collapse Rate, Average Collapse Onset Turn, and Over-Refusal Rate.
For the underlying backbone models, the attacker is instantiated with 
Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite at a temperature of 0.7 0.7 to encourage diverse attack vectors, while the judge employs 
GPT-5.1 at a temperature of 0 0 to ensure deterministic and consistent assessments. Each multi-turn dialogue is capped at a maximum of 16 turns, with early termination triggered immediately upon the judge detecting a model collapse. Furthermore, to efficiently measure adversarial robustness, if no collapse is detected by the sixteenth turn, the attack is officially classified as failed, and the corresponding dialogue is recorded as non-collapsed.
For fair comparison, we employ the same configuration (e.g., system prompt) for all baselines and our method during evaluation. We fix the target model temperature as 0 0 for reproducibility, with a maximum generation length for one sample per turn as 1024 tokens. 
 
Evaluation metrics. 
We evaluate each tutor along three complementary dimensions: whether scaffolding collapse occurs, how late collapse occurs when it is unavoidable, and whether the model avoids over-refusal. 
 
Collapse Rate (CR) ( ↓ \downarrow ) measures the fraction of dialogues with at least one collapse over all dialogues. CR is our primary metric because it directly measures whether a tutor preserves Socratic guidance under sustained adversarial pressure. Since any collapse indicates a failure to maintain the intended tutoring mode, a lower CR reflects stronger robustness across multi-turn interactions. 
 
Average Collapse Onset Turn (OT) ( ↑ \uparrow ) measures the average collapse onset turn (the turn that the tutor response collapses) over all collapsed dialogues. When no dialogue collapses, i.e., (CR=0), OT is reported as N/A. We include OT to capture the temporal depth of robustness: even when collapse eventually occurs, a later onset means that the tutor maintains Socratic scaffolding for more turns. Therefore, a higher OT indicates stronger resistance to sustained adversarial pressure. 
 
Over-Refusal Rate (RR) ( ↓ \downarrow ) measures the fraction of dialogues in which the tutor exhibits excessive defensive behavior, such as refusing legitimate learning requests or failing to provide pedagogically useful guidance, over all dialogues. RR serves as a dual-safety metric for CR. A tutor should reduce scaffolding collapse without simply refusing valid tutoring requests. Otherwise, a model could achieve a low CR by avoiding tutoring altogether. A lower RR indicates that the tutor remains helpful and responsive to legitimate learning needs while preserving Socratic constraints.

Competing Methods 
We compare our framework with several representative competing baselines spanning three main defense paradigms: prompt-based defenses, Socratic tutors, and representation-level methods. We implement all baselines under the same evaluation protocol to ensure a fair comparison. 
 
In 
prompt-based defenses , we mainly evaluate how prompts can affect the tutor performance from three perspectives: (i) Socratic-enforced method  ( Puech et al. 2025 ) reinforces Socratic constraints at the system-prompt level, (ii) Few-shot example method  ( Brown et al. 2020 ) adds in-context Socratic tutoring demonstrations to elicit scaffolding behavior, and (iii) Output filtering method  ( Macina et al. 2023 ) applies an external judge to detect collapsed tutor responses and trigger regeneration when collapse is detected. 
 
For 
Socratic tutors , we assess the following models.
 SocraticLM   ( Liu et al. 2024 ) is a supervised fine-tuned Socratic tutoring model used to evaluate whether positive Socratic imitation improves collapse robustness.
 ORPO   ( Hong, Lee, and Thorne 2024 ) is a single-stage preference-alignment method trained on the cleaned UltraFeedback dataset  ( Cui et al. 2023 ) .
 PedagogicalRL   ( Dinucu-Jianu et al. 2025 ) trains a pedagogically aware tutor with online reinforcement learning, jointly optimizing solution correctness and pedagogical reward, serving as an RL-based pedagogical alignment baseline.
 EULER   ( Bonino et al. 2024 ) is a Socratic tutor fine-tuned from 
Phi-3-mini-4k-Instruct , serving as a small-model educational-tutoring baseline.
 EduChat   ( Dan et al. 2023 ) is an education-domain LLM trained via educational corpus pre-training and instruction fine-tuning to enhance capabilities including open-domain question answering, essay assessment, Socratic teaching, and emotional support, serving as a broader educational-domain baseline. 
 
Regarding 
representation-level and adversarial robustness methods, we adapt each method to the pedagogical-collapse setting by treating collapsed responses as undesirable behaviors to be suppressed.
 RMU   ( Li et al. 2024 ) applies representation misdirection to push target-behavior hidden states toward noise-like representation directions.
 Circuit Breakers   ( Zou et al. 2024 ) uses representation rerouting to disrupt internal representation pathways that lead to harmful outputs.
 RepBend   ( Yousefpour et al. 2025 ) bends hidden representations toward safer directions to suppress undesirable behavior.
 LAT   ( Sheshadri et al. 2024 ) perturbs hidden states during training via latent adversarial training to improve model robustness.
 R2D2   ( Mazeika et al. 2024 ) applies dynamic adversarial training, using a continuously updated pool of adversarially generated requests to improve robustness against adversarially induced undesirable behaviors. 
 
Overall, our method differs from these baselines by jointly modeling pedagogical collapse as both a trajectory-level preference-learning problem and a representation-level scaffolding-alignment problem.

Per-collapse-type representation analysis. 
In our model design, we use a single global direction as an approximation. It is intended to capture the dominant shared component that separates scaffold-preserving responses from collapse responses. The global direction is retained in the main method because it is parameter-efficient and does not require a collapse-type classifier at inference time.
Since different collapse types may also induce type-specific shifts, we further evaluate this approximation through per-collapse-type drift analysis and an ablation using type-specific directions.
Fig.  
7 presents the D S2C D_{\mathrm{S2C}} layer-wise curves across five collapse types using 
Qwen3-8B . We observe that drift peaks exist in the middle-to-late layers with τ \tau drift above τ − 2 \tau{-}2 across all five collapse types, but at markedly different strengths: answer leakage and over-explanation produce the highest peaks (answer leakage the narrowest), role drift and no-question collapse are intermediate, and misconception neglect is the weakest and most diffuse. For role drift, the τ − 2 \tau{-}2 curve is already clearly elevated, indicating that role instability drifts earliest—well before the behavioral collapse. The shared mid-layer peak across pedagogically distinct failures supports a single global collapse direction, while their differing magnitudes reveal type-specific structure.

Full Discussion on Related Work 
SPRA sits at the intersection of three threads. 
 
Socratic tutoring evaluation. 
Recent benchmarks move beyond final-answer accuracy toward dialogue-level pedagogy, adaptive feedback, and learner-centered guidance. TutorBench  ( Srinivasa et al. 2025 ) evaluates adaptive explanations, actionable feedback, and hint generation across high-school and AP-level curricula. MathTutorBench  ( Macina et al. 2025 ) assesses math expertise, student understanding tracking, and teacher response generation. GuideEval  ( Liu et al. 2025 ) introduces a three-phase Perception–Orchestration–Elicitation framework for instructional guidance. These benchmarks largely treat pedagogical quality as a turn-level or response-level property rather than as a failure that unfolds across an interaction trajectory. 
 
 
Multi-turn dialogue drift. 
Models can progressively lose track of user intent, prior instructions, or safety-relevant constraints across multi-turn interaction histories. Laban et al. 2025 report substantial performance degradation in multi-turn settings due to premature assumptions and unrecoverable trajectories. MultiChallenge  ( Deshpande et al. 2025 ) identifies four categories of multi-turn failure: instruction retention, inference memory, versioned editing, and self-coherence. Li et al. 2026 show that safety behavior can depend on dialogue state, with harmful trajectories emerging across multi-turn contexts. Scaffolding collapse is a pedagogy-specific instance of this trajectory-level instability. 
 
 
Preference optimization. 
DPO  ( Rafailov et al. 2023 ) learns from chosen–rejected pairs without an explicit reward model, with extensions including ORPO  ( Hong, Lee, and Thorne 2024 ) , negative preference optimization  ( Zhang et al. 2024 ) , and token-level TDPO  ( Zeng et al. 2024 ) . Shi et al. 2024 further extend DPO to multi-turn agent trajectories with length-normalized objectives (DMPO). These methods treat alignment as a response- or token-level property of an individual prompt–response pair, or optimize multi-turn preferences without explicitly coupling them to hidden-state geometry. 
 
 
Representation-level alignment. 
A complementary line of work intervenes directly on hidden states. Representation engineering and contrastive activation addition expose concept-aligned directions in hidden representations  ( Zou et al. 2023 ; Rimsky et al. 2024 ) . ReFT  ( Wu et al. 2024 ) learns low-rank hidden-state interventions, and SaLoRA  ( Li et al. 2025 ) preserves safety alignment under LoRA adaptation. These methods operate on individual inputs rather than dialogue trajectories. 
 
 
Positioning of SPRA. 
SPRA combines both algorithmic threads through trajectory-weighted preference learning—distinguishing our trajectory-level TDPO from the token-level variant  ( Zeng et al. 2024 ) —and a margin-preserving representation objective anchored to a frozen warmup reference cache, thereby constraining the temporal geometry of Socratic scaffolding rather than only per-input behavior. 
 
 
 
Other Statements 
Potential risk. 
This work studies adversarial pressure against LLM-based Socratic tutors in order to improve their robustness. A potential risk is that the attack strategies used for evaluation could be misused to induce tutoring systems to reveal direct answers or abandon pedagogical guidance. To reduce this risk, our red-teaming scenarios are restricted to benign educational settings and do not involve harmful, illegal, or unsafe content. The attack prompts are used only for evaluation and defense development. We also emphasize that automated Socratic tutors should be deployed with appropriate human oversight, especially in educational contexts. 
 
 
Scientific artifacts used. 
We use publicly available educational datasets and open-weight language models for research purposes. The tutoring data are constructed from MathDial, Socratic Debugging Benchmark, and TutorChat, covering mathematics, computer science, physics, chemistry, and biology. We also use open-weight LLMs as tutor models in our experiments. API-based models are used only as attacker or judge agents in the automated red-teaming framework. All artifacts are used in accordance with their intended research purposes and applicable licenses or terms of use. We do not collect private student data or personally identifiable information. 
 
 
Reproducibility and artifact release. 
Upon publication, we will release datasets, source code, and experimental protocol on GitHub. 
 
 
Computational budgets. 
The experiments were conducted on eight NVIDIA A100 GPUs and one NVIDIA H200 GPU. All conducted experiments required at least 2,400 GPU-hours. 
 
 
AI assistants in writing. 
AI assistants were used to support language polishing, grammar correction, and clarity improvement during manuscript preparation. All scientific ideas, experimental designs, implementation decisions, empirical results, analyses, and final claims were developed, checked, and approved by the authors. 
 
 
Ethical and broader impacts. 
We confirm that we fulfill the author’s responsibilities and address the potential ethical issues.
Broadly speaking, this work may contribute to safer and more reliable AI tutoring systems by reducing the risk that Socratic tutors reveal direct answers or abandon pedagogical guidance under student pressure. More robust tutoring agents could better support independent reasoning and learning, but they should still be deployed with human oversight.
